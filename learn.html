🏁 Financial freedom

You are financially free when your passive income — money that arrives whether or not you work — covers your living expenses.

Most people trade time for money: no work, no paycheck. Financial freedom (often called financial independence, or "FI") flips that around. Instead of working for money, you own things that pay you: rental properties, businesses, dividend-paying stocks, bonds, CDs and loans you've made to others.

Two ways to get there

  • Grow passive income by buying assets that pay you every month.
  • Shrink expenses. Every dollar you cut from your monthly bills is a dollar of passive income you no longer need. Cutting expenses works twice: it frees up cash to invest and lowers the finish line.

How much is "enough"?

A popular rule of thumb in the real world is the 4% rule: if you can live on 4% of your investments per year, your money has historically lasted 30 years or more. That means you need about 25× your yearly expenses invested.

Example. You spend $4,000 a month ($48,000 a year). 25 × $48,000 = $1.2 million invested. Cut spending to $3,000 a month and you need only $900,000 — $300,000 less.

You win when passive income reaches 2× your total monthly expenses (living costs + children + debt payments). The extra margin is a safety cushion: rentals sit empty, businesses have bad months and dividends get cut. Paying off a debt lowers your expenses and your target.

💵 Cashflow

Cashflow = money in − money out, every month. Positive cashflow is the fuel you invest; negative cashflow slowly drains you.

Cashflow is the single most important number in personal finance, because everything else depends on it. A high salary with even higher spending leaves you with nothing to invest. A modest salary with low expenses can build real wealth.

Savings rate

Your savings rate is cashflow ÷ income. It tells you how fast you're moving toward freedom, regardless of how much you earn.

🧾 Financial statements

An income statement shows the money flowing in and out over a period. A balance sheet is a snapshot of what you own and owe at one moment.

Every company publishes these, and they work just as well for a person. Together they answer two different questions: "Am I getting ahead each month?" and "What has it all added up to?"

The income statement

  • Earned income — salary and side gigs. Stops when you stop working.
  • Passive income — rent, business profits, dividends and interest. Keeps coming.
  • Expenses — living costs, family costs, and debt payments.
  • Net cashflow — what's left. This is what you can invest.

Investments are usually shown net: a rental's rent minus its operating expenses and mortgage payment. A $2,000 rent with $700 of expenses and a $1,100 mortgage payment only puts $200 in your pocket.

Interest vs. principal

A loan payment has two parts. The interest is the lender's fee — a true cost. The principal pays back what you borrowed, so it's really moving money from your pocket into lower debt (your net worth doesn't drop). Early in a loan most of the payment is interest; by the end it's nearly all principal.

The balance sheet

Net worth = Assets − Liabilities

Your home counts toward net worth but not toward passive income — it doesn't pay you rent. A new car is an asset on paper, but it loses value every year and its loan is a liability that costs you every month.

⚖️ Assets vs. liabilities

A useful everyday definition: an asset puts money into your pocket; a liability takes money out.

Accountants define assets as anything of value you own. But for building freedom, what matters is cashflow. A boat you own outright is technically an asset — but with fuel, storage, insurance and repairs, it costs you every month. A small rental that nets $250 a month pays you.

  • Income-producing assets: rentals, businesses, dividend stocks, bonds, CDs, lending notes.
  • Growth assets: stocks that pay no dividend, land, gold, crypto, collectibles. They can make you richer when you sell, but don't pay the bills along the way.
  • Liabilities: credit cards, car loans, student loans, personal loans — and things that cost upkeep.

Wealth grows when your cashflow buys assets. It stalls when your cashflow buys liabilities.

Everyday money

🛟 Emergency fund

Keep 3–6 months of expenses in cash you can reach quickly.

Car repairs, medical bills and layoffs aren't rare — they're a normal part of life. Without savings, every surprise lands on a credit card at 20–28% interest, and one bad month can snowball into years of debt.

  • Start with a small goal ($1,000) and build toward 3 months, then 6.
  • More if your income is irregular, you're the only earner, or you have kids.
  • Keep it boring and reachable: a savings account, or part of it in short CDs (3–6 months) if you accept a small penalty to break one.

Layoffs last 1–6 months, and surprise bills pop up about one turn in four. The Money coach warns you when you have less than 3 months of expenses in cash.

📈 Lifestyle creep

When income rises, spending tends to rise right along with it — so you never actually get ahead.

A raise feels like a reason to upgrade: a nicer apartment, a newer car, more dinners out. Each upgrade seems small, but together they quietly absorb the raise. That's why some people with big salaries still live paycheck to paycheck.

A simple defense: when your income goes up, decide in advance how much of the raise you'll save (say, at least half) before it hits your checking account.

Example. Cutting $300 a month of expenses doesn't just save $3,600 a year. In this game it lowers your freedom target by $600/mo (at 2×) — the same as buying an asset that pays $600 a month.

Luxury temptations show up about one turn in four, more often for lavish lifestyles. The Debt & Budget tab lets you downshift to a cheaper lifestyle.

💼 Your earning power

Early in life, your ability to earn is usually your biggest asset.

A 3% raise sounds small, but it repeats every year for the rest of your career and compounds just like investments do. Skills, certifications, a professional network and a good reputation — together called human capital — are investments that pay off for decades.

  • Raises are small permanent bumps; promotions are bigger jumps that come with new responsibilities.
  • Networking costs time and money up front but makes opportunities and new jobs easier to find.
  • Student loans are an investment in earning power — worth it when the degree raises your income by much more than the payments.

💞 Money & relationships

Sharing a household is a big financial advantage; weddings, children and divorce are some of life's biggest expenses.

Two people don't need two kitchens, two internet bills or two sets of furniture, so a couple's household costs much less than twice a single person's. But a lavish wedding can cost a year of savings, raising a child costs thousands a year, and divorce brings legal fees and splits savings in half. Money disagreements are one of the most common sources of relationship stress — talking about money goals early helps.

A spouse pays half of the household bills (including the home mortgage), parents split children's costs, and a divorce costs legal fees plus half of your cash, stocks and CDs.

Debt

💳 Interest, APR & APY

Interest is the price of borrowing money. APR is its yearly rate. APY adds in the effect of compounding.

APR — annual percentage rate

The yearly interest rate on a loan. Credit cards charge interest monthly at APR ÷ 12 on your balance.

Example. A $5,000 card balance at 24% APR costs 2% a month: $100 of interest in the first month alone.

APY — annual percentage yield

APY includes compounding — interest earning interest. It's the honest way to compare savings accounts and CDs. 5% APR compounded monthly is about 5.12% APY.

APY = (1 + APR ÷ n)n − 1   (n = compounding periods per year)

The minimum-payment trap

Credit-card minimums are usually around 1–3% of the balance. Paying only the minimum keeps you in debt for years — most of every payment goes to interest. Paying off a 25% card is like earning a guaranteed 25% return, better than almost any investment.

❄️ Paying off debt: avalanche vs. snowball

The avalanche pays the highest rate first (cheapest overall). The snowball pays the smallest balance first (quick wins).

  • Avalanche: make minimum payments on everything, then throw every extra dollar at the highest-APR debt. Mathematically, this saves the most interest.
  • Snowball: pay off the smallest balance first, then roll its payment into the next. Each payoff frees up a whole monthly payment, which builds momentum and motivation.

Both work far better than paying minimums forever. Extra payments on a fixed loan (car, student, mortgage) usually don't lower the monthly payment — they finish the loan sooner and save interest. Paying down a credit card does lower its minimum payment.

Every debt you pay off removes its payment from your expenses and lowers your freedom target by 2× that payment.

🏗️ Good debt vs. bad debt

"Good" debt buys something that earns more than the debt costs. "Bad" debt buys things that lose value.

  • Usually good: a mortgage on a rental whose rent covers the payment with money left over; a business loan for equipment that produces profit; education that raises your pay.
  • Usually bad: vacations, gadgets and shopping on a credit card; an expensive car loan; a boat.

"Good" debt still has risk: if the rental sits empty or the business has a bad year, the payment is still due. Lenders check your debt-to-income ratio (debt payments ÷ gross income) — many won't lend above about 36–43%.

Safe savings

❄️ Compound growth

When your earnings are reinvested, they start earning money of their own. Over long periods, this snowball is enormous.

Future value = P × (1 + r)t

where P is what you start with, r the yearly return and t the number of years. The Rule of 72 is a handy shortcut: divide 72 by your yearly return to estimate how many years it takes your money to double. At 8%, about 9 years; at 4%, about 18.

Example. $10,000 at 8% a year grows to about $21,600 in 10 years, $46,600 in 20 and $100,600 in 30. The last 10 years add more than the first 20 combined — that's compounding.

Compounding works against you too: credit-card interest compounds, which is why balances can balloon.

🏦 CDs (certificates of deposit)

A CD is a deal with a bank: you leave a set amount with it for a fixed term, and it pays you a fixed, guaranteed rate.

How a CD works

  • Term: how long your money is locked up — commonly 3 months to 5 years.
  • APY: the yearly yield, including compounding. It's fixed for the whole term, even if rates change.
  • Minimum deposit: often $500–$1,000; special "promo" CDs may require more.
  • Maturity: at the end of the term you get your money back plus interest. Many banks automatically roll it into a new CD unless you tell them otherwise.
  • Interest payout: some CDs pay interest out monthly; others let it compound inside the CD until maturity.

The trade-off: safety and a fixed rate vs. access

Bank deposits are insured by the government — in the U.S., by the FDIC (or the NCUA for credit unions) up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. So a CD can't lose money… unless you need it early. Cashing out before maturity triggers an early-withdrawal penalty, typically 3 months of interest for short CDs and 6–12 months for longer ones. Break a CD very early and the penalty can eat into your deposit.

Why rates differ

  • The central bank rate. CD rates rise and fall with it.
  • The bank. Online banks and credit unions often pay far more than big branch banks for the exact same insured deposit. Shopping around is free money.
  • The term — the yield curve. Normally, longer terms pay more as a reward for locking money up. But when rates are high and expected to fall, the curve inverts: short CDs pay more than long ones, because banks don't want to promise today's high rate for years.

CD ladders

Instead of one big CD, split your savings across several that mature at different times — say, 3, 6, 12 and 24 months. When one matures, either use the money or reinvest it at the long end. A ladder gives you regular access to some cash while still capturing longer-term rates, and it means you never have to guess the perfect moment to lock in.

Example. $20,000 split into four $5,000 CDs at 1, 2, 3 and 4 years. Every year one matures; reinvest it in a new 4-year CD. After three years, all four are 4-year CDs (earning the higher long-term rate) — and one still matures every year.

Where CDs fit

CDs are a great home for money you'll need on a known date (a down payment next year) or for the part of your savings you want to keep completely safe. They're not a road to riches: after inflation and taxes, CD returns are modest. Rentals, businesses and stocks can earn much more — with much more risk.

🏛️ Interest rates & bonds

When interest rates rise, existing bonds lose value — and when rates fall, they gain.

A bond is a loan to a government or company that pays a fixed interest rate. Imagine you own a bond paying 3%, and new bonds start paying 5%. Nobody will pay full price for your 3% bond anymore, so its price falls until its yield matches the market.

Duration measures how sensitive a bond is to rate changes: a fund with a duration of 6 falls roughly 6% for each 1-percentage-point rise in rates. The silver lining: as old bonds mature, the fund buys new ones at the higher rates, so its payouts rise.

Who sets interest rates?

A central bank sets a short-term benchmark rate. It cuts rates to stimulate a slow economy and raises them to cool inflation. Mortgage rates, credit-card rates, loan rates and CD rates all move with it.

The central bank rate is in the top bar. It drives mortgage rates, bank-loan rates, CD rates, the bond fund's price and the REIT's price.

Investing

🗽 Stocks & index funds

A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. An index fund owns a slice of hundreds of companies at once.

When a company grows its profits, its shares tend to become more valuable, and many companies share profits with owners as dividends. But any single company can stumble: a failed product, a scandal, a new competitor.

An index fund spreads your money across a whole market — for example, 500 of the largest companies. Its fees are tiny, it never needs a stock-picker, and over long periods most professional fund managers fail to beat it after fees. For many people, a low-cost index fund is the core of a long-term portfolio.

The Liberty 500 Index Fund is the game's diversified fund: about 15% yearly volatility, steady long-run growth and a small, growing dividend.

💸 Dividends & yield

Dividends are profits paid to shareholders. Yield = yearly dividends ÷ price.

Dividend yield = annual dividend per share ÷ share price

Example. A stock pays $2 a year per share and trades at $50: a 4% yield. $10,000 invested pays about $400 a year — $33 a month.
  • Healthy companies tend to raise dividends over time, so your income grows even if you never buy another share.
  • A very high yield can be a warning sign: the price may have fallen because investors expect a dividend cut.
  • Growth companies often pay little or nothing, reinvesting profits instead. They can make you richer when you sell, but they don't pay the bills.

🎢 Volatility & risk

Volatility measures how much a price swings. Higher expected returns usually come with bigger swings.

Volatility is usually given as the standard deviation of yearly returns. If a fund averages 8% a year with 15% volatility, about two years in three land between −7% and +23%, and about one year in twenty is worse than −17%.

  • Drawdown: the fall from a peak to a later low. Even broad stock markets have fallen 30–50% in severe crashes.
  • Volatility clusters: wild months tend to follow wild months, and calm follows calm.
  • Fat tails: extreme moves happen more often than a simple bell curve predicts.

Risk isn't just volatility. It's also the chance you'll be forced to sell at a bad time — which is why an emergency fund and avoiding too much debt make you a better investor.

🧺 Diversification

Own many different investments, so no single disaster can sink you.

The key is correlation — whether investments move together (+1), independently (0) or in opposite directions (−1). Combining things that don't move in lockstep smooths out the ride without giving up much return. Gold and high-quality bonds have often held up when stocks fell.

Diversify across companies, industries, asset types (stocks, bonds, real estate, cash) and income sources. Having all your wealth in your employer's stock, or in one rental in one neighborhood, is concentrated risk.

🔄 Economic cycles

Economies alternate between expansions and recessions.

  • Expansion / boom: jobs are plentiful, profits rise, stocks tend to climb. Cyclical companies (cars, tech, travel) do best.
  • Recession: layoffs rise, profits fall, stocks drop — cyclical ones hardest. Defensive sectors (groceries, healthcare, utilities), bonds and gold tend to hold up better.

Markets look ahead: stocks often fall before a recession is official and recover while the news still feels gloomy. Recessions also create bargains for people with cash ready.

😨 Fear & greed

Prices aren't moved only by facts. Waves of fear and greed push them further than the news alone would — and those mood-driven moves tend to reverse.

The Fear & Greed index

A well-known real-world Fear & Greed Index (published by CNN Business) boils investor mood down to one number from 0 to 100, built from seven market signals:

  • Market momentum — the stock market vs. its average of the last 125 trading days.
  • Stock price strength — how many stocks are hitting 52-week highs vs. lows.
  • Stock price breadth — how much trading volume is flowing into rising vs. falling stocks.
  • Put and call options — whether investors are buying more protection (puts) or more bets on gains (calls).
  • Junk bond demand — how much extra yield investors demand to hold risky bonds.
  • Market volatility — the VIX, the market's "fear gauge".
  • Safe-haven demand — stocks' returns vs. safer Treasury bonds.

How emotions move markets

  • Herding and FOMO: when prices rise, people pile in so they don't miss out, which pushes prices higher still. Taken far enough, this becomes a bubble.
  • Loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains feel good, so falling prices can trigger panic selling.
  • Overconfidence: after a run of gains, investors feel invincible, borrow more and take bigger bets.
  • Flight to safety: in fearful markets, money rushes out of stocks and into bonds, gold and cash.

Why it matters to you

Because mood-driven moves tend to fade, following the crowd usually means buying high and selling low. Warren Buffett's famous advice is to be "fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful". That doesn't mean timing the market perfectly — nobody can — but it does mean:

  • In extreme greed, check your diversification and resist going all-in on whatever is hottest.
  • In extreme fear, don't panic-sell good investments. Keep investing steadily if you can; that's when bargains appear.
  • Decide your plan when you're calm, so you're not making decisions in the heat of the moment. Dollar-cost averaging — investing the same amount every month — takes emotion out of the timing.

The Fear & Greed index in the top bar (and on the Stocks tab) runs from 0 to 100. It climbs when prices rise and news is exciting, and falls after drops, crashes and scary headlines. Greed gives stocks an extra push up (crypto and speculative stocks most), calms volatility, and makes bubbles and crashes more likely. Fear pushes stocks down, bonds and gold up, and makes swings bigger. Moves driven by mood fade over the following months, so — as in the real world — the year after extreme fear has tended to be better for stocks than the year after extreme greed. Easy bots follow the crowd: they panic-sell in fear and chase in greed.

🎰 Speculation vs. investing

Investing means buying something for the income or value it produces. Speculating means betting on a price move.

Buying a rental for its rent, or an index fund for the profits of 500 companies, is investing. Buying a penny stock because of a tip, or a cryptocurrency because it doubled last month, is speculating. Speculation can pay off — but it isn't a plan, and the losers often outnumber the winners. If you speculate, keep it to a small slice you can afford to lose.

Watch out for hot tips and rumors: by the time you hear one, it's often already priced in — or untrue.

Real estate & business

🏠 Leverage

Leverage is using borrowed money to control a bigger asset. It magnifies gains and losses.

Example. You buy a $200,000 property with $20,000 down (10%) and a $180,000 mortgage. If its value rises 10% to $220,000, your $20,000 has become $40,000 of equity — a 100% gain. If it falls 10%, your equity is wiped out.

Leverage also adds a fixed monthly payment. If the property sits empty, the mortgage is still due. Modest leverage on assets with strong, steady cashflow is how many fortunes are built; heavy leverage on shaky assets is how many are lost.

🏡 Rent vs. buy

Neither is always better. Compare the full monthly cost — and what your cash could earn elsewhere.

  • Buying builds equity, fixes your payment and can grow in value, but costs a down payment, closing costs (~2–5%), property taxes, insurance, maintenance (~1% of value a year) and about 6% to sell.
  • Renting is flexible, the landlord pays for repairs, and your down payment stays free to invest.
  • Your home is part of your net worth, but it doesn't pay you passive income — it costs money every month.

A common guideline is to keep housing costs under about 28–30% of your gross income.

Advanced

🎯 Options

An option is a contract giving the right — but not the obligation — to buy or sell 100 shares at a set price before a set date.

  • Call: the right to buy at the strike price. Profits if the stock rises well above the strike.
  • Put: the right to sell at the strike price. Profits if the stock falls, or insures shares you own.
  • Strike: the agreed price. Expiry: the last day. Premium: what the option costs.
  • Intrinsic value: what it's worth if exercised now. Time value: the rest of the premium — the chance it gains before expiry. Time value melts away as expiry approaches ("time decay").
  • Straddle: a call and a put at the same strike — a bet on a big move in either direction.

The Black–Scholes formula prices options from five inputs: the stock price, the strike, the time left, interest rates and — most important — volatility. More volatility means a bigger chance of a large move, so options cost more.

Example. A stock trades at $100. A 3-month call with a $100 strike costs $4 per share ($400 per contract). If the stock is $112 at expiry, the call is worth $12 ($1,200): a 200% gain. If it's $100 or below, the call expires worthless and the $400 is gone.

Options are leverage: a small premium controls a lot of stock. Most options bought by individuals expire worthless — keep them a small slice of your investments.

🔻 Short selling

Shorting is borrowing shares, selling them now, and buying them back later — profiting if the price falls.

  • Unlimited risk: a stock can only fall to $0, but it can rise without limit — so can a short seller's losses.
  • Margin: brokers require collateral and charge a borrow fee; you also owe any dividends paid while you're short.
  • Margin call: if the price rises too far, the broker demands more collateral or forces you to buy back at a loss.
  • Short squeeze: when many short sellers rush to buy back at once, their buying drives the price even higher.

Loss aversion
The tendency to feel losses about twice as strongly as equal gains, which can lead to panic selling.
LTV
Loan-to-value ratio: loan amount ÷ property value.
Margin call
A broker's demand for more collateral when a leveraged or short position moves against you.
Maturity
The date a CD, bond or loan ends and the principal is repaid.
Net worth
Everything you own minus everything you owe.
NOI
Net operating income: a property's rent minus its operating expenses, before any loan payments.
Passive income
Income that arrives without you working for it: rent, dividends, business profits, interest.
Principal
The amount borrowed or deposited, not counting interest.
Put option
The right to sell 100 shares at the strike price before expiry.
Recession
A broad decline in economic activity, usually with rising unemployment.
REIT
Real estate investment trust: a company that owns property and pays out most of its profits as dividends.
Rule of 72
72 ÷ yearly return ≈ years for money to double.
Savings rate
The share of your income you keep: cashflow ÷ income.
Short selling
Selling borrowed shares, hoping to buy them back cheaper later.
Standard deviation
A measure of how spread out returns are around their average — the usual way to express volatility.
Stock split
Turning each share into several cheaper ones. Your total value doesn't change.
Strike price
The price at which an option lets you buy (call) or sell (put).
Term
How long a CD, bond or loan lasts.
VIX
A measure of expected stock-market volatility, often called the market's "fear gauge".
Volatility
How much a price swings up and down.
Yield
Income from an investment as a percentage of its price.
Yield curve
A chart of interest rates by length of time. Normally upward-sloping; sometimes flat or inverted.
